# Roman Aqueducts

The aqueducts of ancient Rome were engineered channels that carried fresh water from distant springs and rivers into cities and towns. They were among Rome's most important public works, feeding drinking fountains, bathhouses, and private homes.

Aqueducts moved their water entirely by gravity, with no pumps anywhere in the system. Engineers laid each channel along a gentle, unbroken downward slope so the water flowed steadily from source to destination, sometimes across many kilometers. Despite the impression left by the famous arches, most of an aqueduct's length actually ran underground in covered conduits; the arched bridges were used mainly to carry the channel across valleys and low ground while holding that slope.

The first aqueduct to serve the city was the Aqua Appia, built in 312 BC under the censor Appius Claudius Caecus, and many more followed over the centuries. They were made of stone, brick, and durable Roman concrete, with the water channels often lined to keep them watertight. One of the best-preserved survivors is the Pont du Gard in southern France, a tall, multi-tiered bridge that once carried the channel supplying Nîmes across a river valley.
